Abstract

The Smart Home League is an educational robotics competition that introduces students to the world of the smart home and the Internet of Things (IoT): competitors write programs for autonomous robots inside a virtual smart house. The goal is to build algorithmic thinking, programming, problem solving and teamwork. The league has several branches, and Smart Vacuum — the one this rulebook covers — is one of them. The Smart Home League runs within FIRA competitions and has been held at FIRA Iran and Canada.

Everything runs entirely virtually, inside the league's own official simulator — the same program you received in your competition folder. The robot, the house, the sensors, the automatic refereeing and the match report all live in it. The robot must cross several rooms on its own, avoid obstacles, manage its battery in U19, and clean as much of the house as it can.

This booklet is the final authority on the rules and is written from the actual behaviour of the game engine: every number you read here is the number the code runs. Version 4.0 supersedes 3.1 — the relocation rule, the competition structure (Swiss and round-robin), draws and overtime, and every field number have been rewritten. Clauses of 3.1 that do not exist in the simulator — the Cleaning % × K formula, "Boost Stations", "GPS jamming" and "Light View" — have been removed and are no longer in force.

2 · The robot and its sensors

The robots are autonomous virtual vacuum cleaners, and they are ready-made in all three divisions — nobody builds a robot. A team picks a colour and writes the brain.

2.1 What the robot can do

  • Autonomy: the robot runs on the team's own Python program and nothing else.
  • Driving: by setting the left and right wheel speeds (wheelleft / wheelright, range −25..25): forward, backward, turn.
  • Ready-made moves: forward(s), backward(s), turnleft(s), turnright(s), stop(s) — the number is real seconds.
  • Mission-driven design: the most cleaning in a limited time; efficiency and avoiding collisions are what score.
  • Energy management (U19 only): a simulated battery your algorithm has to account for.

3 · The competition house

Figure 4 — the official field seen from straight above
  • 3.1 The field: a house of 22×22 tiles at 62.5 cm per tile (about 13.75 × 13.75 m): hall, kitchen, three bedrooms and a bathroom. All three divisions play this same house; what differs between them is the sensors and which rules are switched on, not the map.
  • 3.2 Starting corners: Team 1 (red) bottom-left, Team 2 (blue) top-right. The in-game coin flip before each match decides which team is Team 1.
  • 3.3 What scores: open floor only. Rugs and the space under furniture are never counted.
  • 3.4 Rugs: the green rug halves your speed and scores nothing. The purple, orange and cyan rugs are doorway markers only: full speed, no points.
  • 3.5 The door: on every standard map one room has a door. A door is not a wall: distance sensors see straight through it, and it opens by itself the moment a robot reaches it — no pushing, no collision recorded. About 8 s later, if no robot is in the frame, it closes again.
  • 3.6 Pets (U14 and U19): a cat and a dog wander the house as moving obstacles; they are off in FS. In FINAL mode the cat erases painted tiles and takes the point back off their owner.
  • 3.7 Water on the floor (U19 only): four puddles — centred at (6.88, 10.31), (6.88, 3.44), (11.56, 6.56) and (2.81, 7.19), each about one tile in radius — are drawn on the floor from the first second and appear on the printed map. Every fresh entry into water costs 10 % of the battery (never tiles); standing in it costs nothing further. FS and U14 have no water at all.
  • 3.8 The charging pad (U19 only): it has a fixed place on the map and its coordinates are given to both robots (dockx, docky).
  • 3.9 Every object in the house is dynamic and may move from one event to the next — but within a round the map and the settings are identical for every team.

4 · Scoring, the end of a match, and draws

  1. Every cleaned floor tile = 1 point.
  2. Last touch owns the tile: driving over the rival's tile takes it from them. This is on in all three divisions and no division turns it off.
  3. The scoreboard also shows the house percentage: how much of the house has been cleaned.
  4. The match ends when the clock does; whoever owns more tiles wins.
  5. A draw does not end the match. Overtime is added in this ladder:
+10 s , then +5 , +5 , +5 , +5 , +5  —  six periods at most, 35 s in total

If it is still level after all of that, the result stands as a draw. In knockout games a draw is not accepted: after the same ladder, sudden death continues in +5 s steps until one team goes ahead (§7.4). The referee's End Game grants no overtime — the result is final at that instant.

5 · Penalties and referee intervention

5.1 Relocation — the core refereeing rule

EventConditionCost
Stuck (automatic)The robot stays 15 s inside a circle of 0.85 m radius — spinning on the spot countsIt is moved to a random free tile anywhere in the house, at least 6 tiles away from where it got stuck, plus the fine
Manual referee relocationThe Relocate button in the ☰ menuExactly the same fine

The fine climbs, and is counted separately for each robot:

first time −5 tiles  ·  second time −10 tiles  ·  third and every one after it −12 tiles
  • These numbers are the same in all three divisions, and there is no penalty-free relocation — manual or automatic makes no difference.
  • The fine always comes off the team's own painted floor: that many tiles turn white again and the score drops. A team that does not have that many tiles simply loses what it has.
  • There is no cap on relocations and a team does not have to "request" anything: the simulator's watchdog acts on its own and picks the destination itself. The 3-relocation cap and the "−5 % of the total score" penalty from version 3.1 are withdrawn.
  • Two exceptions: a robot that is taking on charge has its stuck clock paused — but it restarts the moment the battery is full, because camping on the pad is not cleaning; and a low robot queueing beside the pad is waiting in line, not stuck.
  • A flat battery is not being stuck: a crawling robot is neither moved nor fined.
  • The landing spot is always a fully open tile and is generated from the match seed (§10.2) — so it is reproducible and can be checked in an appeal.

5.2 The battery (U19 only)

  • battery starts at 100; driving flat out burns about 1.7 % per second (100 % ≈ 60 s at full speed).
  • On the charging pad it refills at about 25 % per second.
  • At zero the robot does not die, but it only crawls at 15 % speed — it can drag itself to the pad, though crawling across the house eats most of the time left. The referee will not rescue it; this is the game's own rule.
  • The dust bin and the emptying station are switched off for this season's matches and the bin never fills.

5.3 The referee's other controls

Stop, End Game, Restart and 2× speed — all in the ☰ menu. Each is recorded in the report with the match time at which it was used.

7 · Competition structure: how many teams, which format

A division is run in one of two official shapes, and the organiser announces which before it starts:

  • Head-to-head: teams play each other in pairs and a table is formed — Swiss, a single round-robin or a double round-robin. This is the main shape of the competition.
  • Solo rounds: each team plays alone in the house and its cleaning percentage is recorded; the sum of the rounds gives the ranking (§7.6).

7.1 Which format for how many teams — the official table

One match takes about 8 to 10 minutes of arena time (setting up, loading two programs, the match itself, recording the result), so one arena fits about 40 matches in a competition day. A full round-robin needs n×(n−1)÷2 matches, and that number is what draws the line: up to 8 teams a full round-robin fits in a day and is the fairest format there is, because every team plays everyone and the draw has no influence on the result at all. From 9 teams upward a round-robin no longer fits (9 teams = 36 matches, 12 = 66, 16 = 120), and Swiss is the right replacement: the number of matches stays fixed, nobody is eliminated, and from round two on every team meets an opponent on its own level.

The general rule for Swiss rounds: ⌈log₂n⌉ + 1, and never fewer than 4. With ⌈log₂n⌉ rounds exactly one unbeaten team is left standing; the extra round is what stops a single bad match from ending a team's whole event, and it makes the tiebreaks mean something. If the organiser enters no number, the software works out a safe minimum on its own; either way the number announced at the start of the event is binding and does not change mid-competition. The round count never exceeds a full round-robin — past that, every game is a rematch.

7.2 How the Swiss system works

  1. Round 1 is a genuine draw: nobody has a record yet, so the slots are handed out by a cryptographic random draw and the table is folded once — the top half against the bottom half.
  2. From round 2 the table does the drawing: teams on equal points form a "score group", and each group is split down the middle — winners meet winners, losers meet losers. An odd group floats one team down into the next.
  3. Nobody meets the same opponent twice. This is held for as long as the mathematics allows: the system puts a heavy cost on any repeat and builds the cheapest possible round out of all the ones available. Only when the rounds outnumber the opponents a team has not yet met does a rematch become unavoidable.
  4. An odd number of teams means a bye: a bye counts as a win (3 points) but scores no tiles, so its tile difference is zero. The bye goes to the lowest-placed team that has not had one yet, and no team gets a second bye until every team has had one.
  5. The next round is only drawn once every result of the current round has been entered.

7.3 The table, the points and the tiebreaks

ResultTable points
Win (more tiles at the end of normal and overtime)3
Draw (still level after the full 35 s of overtime)1
Loss0
Bye3 — with no tiles

The tiebreaks, in exactly this order:

  1. Table points
  2. Tile difference — tiles scored minus tiles conceded, across the whole competition
  3. Tiles scored
  4. Buchholz — the sum of the points of the opponents you have played (Swiss only)
  5. A draw by the referee, in front of both teams' supervisors
Why Buchholz comes last: in a Swiss that folds the table, the leader keeps being handed the weakest opponents. A high Buchholz therefore marks a team that has been near the bottom, not one that had a hard road — which is why it is not used as the primary tiebreak. Teams level on every sporting criterion share a rank.

7.4 Playoffs and finals

  • Where a playoff is used, the top four teams meet as 1 v 4 and 2 v 3, followed by the final and the third-place match. A top-8 playoff adds a quarter-final round in front.
  • A knockout game cannot be drawn: after the overtime ladder of §4, sudden death continues in +5 s steps until one team goes ahead; only the referee's End Game can cut it short.
  • In a round-robin without a playoff the table decides the champion; if the top two are level on tiebreaks 1 through 4 as well, a single-match final is played.

7.5 Fairness rules that hold in every format

  • One map per round: every match of a round is played on the same map with the same settings.
  • Corners by coin: red/blue and bottom-left/top-right are decided by the in-game coin flip, never by table position. In a best-of-3 final the corners are swapped for the second game.
  • The order of play is drawn: in a round-robin the seats, the order of the rounds and the order of the matches within a round are all shuffled — the fixture list cannot be predicted in advance.
  • Withdrawal: a team that withdraws is taken off the table; the results its opponents already earned stay with them.
  • Failing to appear: a team not at the arena by the end of the referee's call loses that match.

7.6 Solo rounds (the second shape)

If an event is run in solo rounds instead of head to head, each team plays alone for several rounds and the cleaning percentage of each round is recorded. The ranking is the sum of the rounds, and every field rule — relocation, fines, battery, water — is exactly the same. The organiser announces before the event how many rounds there are and whether the worst one is dropped.

7.7 The super-team event (optional)

If time allows, teams join into "super teams" and solve a shared mission — announced on the day — in a 240-second match. The winning super team receives a certificate of honour. The super-team result has no effect on the divisions' competition tables.

8 · Technical challenge, technical interview and the event total

A team's final standing at an event is built from three parts; the organiser announces the exact weight of each before the event:

PartWhat is measured
8.1 CompetitionThe team's place in the table and playoff of §7
8.2 Technical challengeMissions separate from ordinary cleaning, to measure programming skill and creativity: wall following (around a room at a constant distance from the wall), room-to-room navigation (from room A to room Z with, say, a 10-second stop halfway) and avoiding a moving obstacle
8.3 Technical interviewUnderstanding the code — every member must be able to explain every part of the algorithm clearly — innovation and problem solving, the division of work within the team, and every library or helper tool you used (helpers included) must be explainable by the team itself

9 · Team requirements

9.1 The team

  • 2 to 6 members per team.
  • Robots run only on the team's own Python program, fully autonomously.
  • Following the competition schedule and the referees' instructions is mandatory; so is respect towards teams, referees and organisers throughout the event.

9.2 Handing in the code

  • The source code must be handed in exactly at the announced time before the match; the USB stick goes into the container at the hand-in point.
  • The referee announces the file-name format before each match — usually the round number + team name + .py. Example: team "Sweeper" in round 2 → R2-Sweeper.py
  • Being late, or naming the file wrongly, carries a penalty (§11).

9.3 Quarantine

  • At the start of each quarantine the teams receive the full map for that round.
  • A set amount of time (it varies per event) is given for writing or fixing code in a controlled room.
  • Inside quarantine no communication device (phone, tablet, smart watch, internet) and no outside source is allowed. Entering and leaving are forbidden.

9.4 Equipment and USB

  • A power strip and a fully charged laptop with a working charger.
  • A formatted, ready USB stick; the name on the stick must match the name on the scoreboard; a legible label with the team name; only that round's code on it.

9.5 Coach conduct

  • A coach may not communicate with the students during quarantine; a breach is penalised up to disqualification from that round.
  • Appeals must be written and professional; unprofessional conduct means suspension of the coach or disqualification of the team.

10 · Official operation and the integrity of the result

  1. The official result comes from the referee's computer only. The physics runs on a fixed 1/60 s step and does not depend on the monitor's frame rate; even so, the referee's station is the reference.
  2. The match seed. The referee may enter a numeric seed; left blank, a random one is generated and recorded. "Seed + map + the two code files" reproduces a match exactly — the destination of every relocation comes from that seed too.
  3. Official mode (🔒). Before every official game the referee enables Official mode with a PIN: live editing of the house, 2× speed, Shift+drag dragging and every way of swapping code are locked. This is the code freeze.
  4. Code identity. On loading, the first 12 digits of the file's SHA-256 are shown beside its name and recorded; handling an appeal means comparing those two hashes.
  5. The compute budget. A program that repeatedly burns more than 15 ms per tick gets a referee warning; a repeat is a technical loss of that game.
  6. Program errors. A runtime error zeroes that tick's wheels and is counted; a program that errors on more than 20 % of the ticks is a technical loser at the referee's discretion.
  7. False starts. "Restart match" voids the countdown or the running game and starts the same settings afresh; a voided run is recorded nowhere.
  8. Records and exports. Every match is logged automatically in the match book (CSV export), and each match report — a heat-map PNG plus the full JSON event log — can be downloaded and attached to an appeal.
  9. Changing the algorithm during the event must be reported to the technical committee; otherwise expect a strict interview or even disqualification.
  10. The technical committee is not responsible for teams' own software problems; it only guarantees that at least one laptop per team can run the latest league software.
  11. External software and libraries are allowed — but they fall under the technical interview of §8.3.
